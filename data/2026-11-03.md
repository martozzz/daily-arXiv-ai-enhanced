Abstract: Biological neural systems achieve impressive computational capabilities under tight metabolic constraints, relying on sparse cortical representations that minimise total neural activity. This combination of efficiency and representational power has motivated growing interest in sparse coding as a computational strategy for artificial systems. Artificial models designed to produce binary sparse codes span three broad paradigms, theoretical methods grounded in efficient coding, deep learning methods trained via backpropagation, and biologically plausible methods that learn through local Hebbian rules, each yielding codes whose quality depends not only on achieving low activation density but also on preserving the informational structure of the input. The latter property, however, lacks an agreed-upon quantitative definition, leaving the field without a principled basis for comparing methods. We propose a set of metrics that formalise information preservation as similarity preservation, measuring how faithfully a binary sparse code reproduces the relational geometry of the input space, and apply this framework to compare four representative methods under matched conditions. The results reveal a consistent trade-off in which models that achieve high information preservation do so at the cost of sparsity, while models approaching the uniformity of an optimal random sparse code tend to sacrifice it. Competitive Sparse Codes, a biologically plausible approach, is the only architecture to occupy a region of near-maximal sparsity while retaining meaningful information preservation. These findings frame the simultaneous satisfaction of both factors as an open challenge for the field and provide a reusable evaluation framework for measuring progress toward it.

Abstract: Autonomous inspection of a target spacecraft is typically the initial step in on-orbit servicing. Planning close-proximity observation viewpoints involves a complex combinatorial problem constrained by coverage, fuel, and safety considerations. This paper models the viewpoint selection as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) and applies Proximal Policy Optimization with Lagrangian relaxation to solve it. The system combines a high-level planner, which sequences camera viewpoints on an observation sphere around the target, with a low-level model that assesses each transfer using the Clohessy--Wiltshire (CW) equations for relative motion. The deterministic constraints on single-impulse reachability and $Δv$ cap are enforced directly on the action set. Safety margins are affected by navigation and manoeuvre errors, making the actual clearance stochastic. To address this, a chance constraint on the realised trajectory is implemented, with a Lagrangian multiplier governed by PID control to adapt the penalty during training. The approach is tested on a Galileo-class navigation satellite in medium Earth orbit, modelled as a point cloud where visibility from each viewpoint considers field-of-view and line-of-sight occlusion. Across various risk budgets, the trained policy maintains the keep-out violation rate within the specified limit, achieving coverage close to its maximum and staying within the mission $Δv$ budget. Compared to the usual practice of applying a fixed margin to the keep-out radius and planning deterministically, the chance-constrained policy attains less than half the violation rate at comparable coverage. This makes the collision risk an explicit design parameter, eliminating the need for re-tuning per case. The learned policy selects a viewpoint without the repeated Monte Carlo risk estimates required by a greedy, chance-constrained planner at every decision point.
